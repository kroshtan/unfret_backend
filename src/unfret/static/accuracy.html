Second-hand ads

Ads on Marktplaats, 2dehands, Vinted, eBay, Craigslist and Facebook Marketplace. These verdicts come from rules written from public scam warnings, not yet from a trained model. Every ad here is synthetic: the platforms forbid collecting real ads, so we wrote them from patterns that consumer warnings document (a deposit first, "I'm abroad", WhatsApp only, a price too good to be true) and normal ones. Read these numbers as a check that the rules catch the documented tricks, not as real-world accuracy.

Where the labels come from

Labels come from outside sources, not from our own judgement:

{{block:origins}}

A second, larger language model (not the one Unfret uses) then read every page with written instructions; {{num:corpus.second_opinion_agreed}} of the {{num:corpus.pages}} labels are confirmed by it. Ads, tradespeople and the most recently added shops keep their label only when it confidently agreed. For the research datasets, whose labels came from people, a disagreement leaves the label as it was and puts the page up for review.

How it's split

Each page is assigned once, at random but repeatably, to training (70%: {{num:corpus.split.train}} pages) or testing (30%: {{num:corpus.split.test}}), in the same proportion within each source and label. The scorer for shops and product pages learns only from the training pages. The numbers above cover both, as a replay of every labelled page; the test-only figures are given under the product table.

Replaying recorded pages

Each page was saved once (from the Internet Archive, from a dataset, fetched live, or generated), and the language model's answers about it were recorded. A measurement replays exactly that: the same pages through the same checks as a real check, with the recorded answers. So every run is repeatable and costs nothing. Lookups that would ask today's world about an old page are off: the age of the domain, the EU VAT register, reverse image search and linking shops that share an owner. In real use those add evidence; they aren't counted here.

What these numbers can't tell you

  • Unfret sees the page, not the deal. It can't see whether a parcel arrives, what happens after you pay, a seller's messages, or anything behind a login it isn't shown. A clever scam with a careful page can look fine, and a real shop with a sloppy page can look worrying.
  • Many pages are a few years old. Most real pages are archived copies, many from 2022 and 2023. Scams change; older pages measure older tricks.
  • Europe and the US, unevenly. Where the test pages are from: {{block:markets}}. Shops from elsewhere are rare in the test.
  • Synthetic pages. {{num:corpus.synthetic}} of the {{num:corpus.pages}} pages are generated: the marketplace product pages (built from a public dataset of real products and reviews), all ads, the tradespeople's platform profiles and some of their websites. They test whether Unfret notices documented signs, not whether it finds new ones.
  • Labels can be wrong. A warning list can name a shop that later cleaned up, and an established business can still treat a customer badly.
  • Signals, not verdicts on a business. Unfret tells you what stands out on a page and how careful to be. It doesn't say a business is a scam, and the decision stays yours.

Changes per scorer version

Shops and product pages get their verdict from a small trained scorer; its version is in every result (and the code and the open model are open source). Ads and tradespeople get theirs from rules. Each release says which model it changed: the open model in the code, or the private model on unfret.net. What changed, newest first:

0.13.0, November 18, 2026 (private model on unfret.net: scorer v1-20261118-113117-C, quick checks v1-quick-20261118-113123-C; open model v1-20261109-222323-C unchanged)
The first private model unfret.net runs: retrained, and not published, like the pages it learnt from; self-hosted servers keep the open model. In the replay of every labelled product page it warns about 83% of the unreliable pages (the open model: 73%), and called no legitimate shop Serious concerns (the open model: 1.6%). Fewer legitimate shops get Worth a look (11% instead of 30%), more get Think twice (16% instead of 11%). On the pages held back from its training: 79% of unreliable pages warned about (the open model: 68%), 11% of legitimate pages (the open model: 6.6%). On the 180 product pages of well-known sellers in the test set, a deep check gave 6 of them Worth a look or worse and none Serious concerns (the open model: 24 of the 180, none Serious concerns). A quick check, measured on 179 of those pages, gave 5 of them Worth a look or worse and none Serious concerns (the open model's quick check: 160 of the 179, one of them Serious concerns). With both, a page they put at Worth a look without a concrete reason to show reads Looks fine.
Serious concerns only when the scorer is at least 60% sure; below that the same page shows Think twice, still a warning. We measured that with the open model on the 1,582 product pages the test set had on November 17, 2026: its Serious concerns were right 95% of the time instead of 94%, and went to 15 legitimate pages instead of 25. This release's fixes for well-known sellers then brought that down to 12 pages. On the next day's test set, with 180 well-known sellers' pages added (none of them got Serious concerns), that is 96% right, and the 12 are 1.6% of the 753 legitimate shops' pages: the open model's numbers in the table above when this release came out. Checks for second-hand ads and tradespeople come to everyone. This page.
0.12.0, November 16, 2026 (open model unchanged)
More established US shops and shops on a consumer-authority warning list in the test set, so that "a US shop" or "this source" no longer gives the answer away. A scorer retrained on it warned about more legitimate shops, so it was not shipped.
0.11.0 and 0.11.1, November 15–16, 2026 (open model unchanged)
Checks for second-hand ads and tradespeople, and seven new checks on product pages that show reasons without changing the score. Training now guards against learning the test set instead of shops; a retrain under those rules wasn't better, so it was not shipped.
0.7.0, November 10, 2026 (open model: scorer v1-20261109-222323-C, still the open model today)
A new open model, with fewer false alarms: the scorer no longer assumes most pages are bad (the test set has more unreliable pages than the web does). On the pages it never saw, legitimate pages warned about went from 35 to 24 of 143; unreliable pages caught from 168 to 151 of 190. US shops are no longer marked down for lacking EU company details. Its numbers on today's test set are the open model's in the table above.
0.6.0, November 8, 2026 (open model: scorer v1-20261108-C)
The first trained scorer: a small, readable model over the checks' signals, so each reason's weight in the verdict is exact. Released as open source, as the first open model. Its numbers, measured afterwards on a later test set, are under earlier versions.
